Color Pipeline

A short, hands-on course in colour spaces: what they are, why log footage looks grey, why renders look dark, and how a picture gets from the scene to the screen.

What is a colour space?

Every pixel is just three numbers. On their own they don't mean a colour. A colour space is the agreement that says what colour those numbers stand for.

Move the sliders. The same three numbers turn into three different colours depending on which space you say they're in. Nearly every "why does this look wrong?" problem in post is two pieces of software disagreeing about that.

A colour space is three decisions

1 · PrimariesWhich red, green and blue

The exact colours "full red", "full green" and "full blue" mean. Everything a space can show is a mix of those three: its gamut. A laser green sits outside a small gamut and inside a big one.

2 · White pointWhat counts as white

The colour you get when all three numbers are equal. Most video uses D65, about 6500K daylight. Drag the slider: if the space (or the screen) assumes a different white, every colour in the shot shifts with it.

3 · Transfer curveHow numbers become brightness

A file only has so many steps. Spread them evenly over light (linear) and the shadows band into a few big steps. A log curve spreads them by stops, so shadows stay smooth. Drop the bits and compare.

So "Rec.709" names all three: Rec.709 primaries, a D65 white and a display gamma. "ARRI LogC4 / AWG4" is another: ARRI's own wide primaries, D65 and a log curve.

Two kinds of colour space

Scene-referredThe light in front of the lens

Numbers proportional to real light, with no top limit: a bare bulb can be hundreds of times brighter than a face. Camera log files, EXR renders and ACES work this way. You grade and composite here.

Display-referredWhat a screen should emit

0 is the screen's black and 1 is the brightest it can show. Anything brighter is gone. Rec.709, sRGB and P3 deliver this way. It's where a picture ends up, not where you want to work on it.

Each step on this scale is one stop: twice the light. A screen covers a slice of the real world; the camera holds more; the scene goes further still. Squeezing scene into screen is the pipeline's hardest job.

Gamut: which colours

A gamut is a paint box with three paints. Mix them and you get every colour inside the triangle between them, but nothing outside it.

          Reading the chart in detail

          The horseshoe. The curved edge is pure single-wavelength light, like a laser or a prism's rainbow: violet bottom left, green at the top, red on the right (numbers in nanometres). The straight bottom edge is the purples, which no single wavelength makes. The closer to the middle, the less saturated, until you reach white.

          The triangles. A colour space's three primaries are three points on this map. Join them up and the triangle is its gamut. Your pipeline's are drawn: camera, working space and display (dashed). Change them on the tab.

          Outside a triangle the colour needs a negative amount of a primary, which a screen can't emit. Something has to give: clipped (a hard edge and a hue shift) or mapped (gently pulled inside).

          Corners outside the horseshoe are "imaginary" primaries. No real light looks like that. Camera and working spaces use them on purpose so the triangle can hold every real colour a sensor sees.

          Curves: how bright

          The same picture, stored three ways. Same light, different numbers: only one of them is meant to be looked at.

          Where do the numbers go?

          A 10-bit file has 1,024 values to spend. Each bar is one stop of light (one doubling) and how many values it gets. Linear blows half its budget on the brightest stop and gives the shadows almost nothing. sRGB is fairer, but stops at the screen's white. Log gives every stop a fair share, and keeps going above white.

          The curves themselves

          Left to right is light, in stops; up is the number stored. Drag the exposure and watch the display curves (sRGB, linear) hit the top at display white while the log curves keep climbing.

            The numbers

            Ramps: −8 to +8 stops left to right, each curve's numbers shown straight on screen with no conversion.

            HDR: beyond 0 to 1

            In SDR, 1.0 means "as bright as this screen goes", whatever that is. In HDR, the numbers mean real brightness, measured in nits, up to 10,000.

            SDR · relative0 to 1, scaled to the screen

            Rec.709 is mastered for a white of 100 nits (sRGB's standard says 80). A brighter TV just makes everything brighter, including the white shirt. Nothing in the signal can be brighter than white, so sun glints and lamps sit at the same level as paper.

            HDR · absoluteCode value = nits

            PQ (SMPTE ST 2084) gives every code value a fixed brightness. Paper-white sits around 200 nits, and everything above is headroom for highlights: sun, lamps, neon, reflections. HLG, used in broadcast, is relative like SDR but with headroom built in.

            Log scale: each gridline is 10× brighter. Typical figures; real screens vary.

            Same scene, SDR vs HDR

            HDR10

            PQ, Rec.2020 container, one set of static metadata for the whole programme. The baseline for streaming and UHD Blu-ray.

            HDR10+ · Dolby Vision

            PQ with dynamic metadata, shot by shot, telling each TV how to tone-map to its own peak brightness.

            HLG

            Hybrid Log-Gamma: relative, no metadata, and watchable on SDR sets that accept Rec.2020 colour. Built for live broadcast.

            File formats: what's inside

            A file format is a container. What matters is what it holds: how many bits, which curve, and whether values above 1.0 survive.

            Software

              Hardware

                ACES, demystified

                ACES (the Academy Color Encoding System) is a free, open standard for colour management: an agreed set of colour spaces and conversions, so cameras, CG, VFX and grading can hand pictures to each other without guessing.

                It is

                A shared language: one big scene-referred space everything converts into, standard conversions in from every camera and out to every screen, and a record of which ones were used. Created by the Academy of Motion Picture Arts and Sciences; since 2025 an open-source project of the Academy Software Foundation.

                It isn't

                A look, a LUT, a camera setting or a grading style. ACES has a default way of turning scene light into a picture (the output transform), but your look comes from your grade, just as in any other pipeline.

                The ACES pipeline, one piece at a time

                  The ACES colour spaces

                  Five encodings, two sets of primaries. They all describe the same scene light; they differ in what they're for.

                  Which one do I use?

                    ACES 1.x vs 2.0

                    ACES 1.x turns scene light into a picture in two steps: the RRT (Reference Rendering Transform, a film-like tone curve and colour tweaks, the same for everyone) and an ODT (Output Device Transform, one per kind of screen). Very saturated light, such as LEDs and neon, could clip and skew, so 1.3 added a Reference Gamut Compression step to use first.

                    ACES 2.0 replaces RRT + ODT with one Output Transform built on a model of how we see colour. It maps out-of-gamut colours itself, holds hue better in bright, saturated light, and matches SDR and HDR versions more closely.

                    The pictures on this page use ACES 1.3 (with gamut compression), ported from the Academy's reference code.

                    Pipelines

                      Signals: SDI and ST 2110

                      Files carry a colour tag. Live pictures need one too. On set, in broadcast and in virtual production, video travels as a signal: down an SDI cable, or more and more, across an ordinary computer network using SMPTE ST 2110.

                      SDIOne cable, everything inside

                      Picture, audio and data travel together down one coax cable at fixed speeds (1.5G for HD up to 30p, 3G for 1080p50/60, 12G for UHD at 50/60p). The signal's colour space rides along as a few flags in its payload ID (SMPTE ST 352), which can say Rec.709 or Rec.2020, and SDR, HLG or PQ.

                      SMPTE ST 2110Separate streams on a network

                      Video, audio and data each become their own stream of network packets over standard Ethernet, synced by a shared network clock (PTP). Each stream is described by a short text file, an SDP, and that's where its colour space is written down.

                      Colour in the stream: build an SDP line

                      A 2110 video stream's SDP has a line of format parameters. These choices are the ones that decide how the receiver turns numbers back into colour.
